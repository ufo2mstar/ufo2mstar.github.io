There's a moment every new parent eventually faces - the quiet desperation of needing five minutes, the glowing rectangle nearby, and a small human who has not yet developed opinions. You press play on something. Anything. And then, months later, you realize you've been curating a media diet for a developing brain, whether you intended to or not.

With our first child, that realization came gradually. With our newborn, we're thinking about it from day one.

## The Landscape Has Changed - Mostly for the Better

Children's entertainment has always been a mixed bag, but the last decade has produced something genuinely encouraging: a small cohort of creators and studios who seem to actually care about what's happening inside a toddler's head.

The clearest example of this wave is **[Miss Rachel](https://www.youtube.com/@MissRachelSongsforLittles)** - Songs for Littles on YouTube. What started as a deeply personal project, a music teacher and her husband documenting play-based learning for their own child with a speech delay, became a phenomenon. And it became one for the right reasons. The pacing is slow and deliberate. Miss Rachel addresses the camera the way you'd want a kind adult to address your child: with patience, eye contact, and genuine warmth. She introduces concepts - colors, words, emotions - by repeating them, singing them, waiting. The music is simple and melodic without being grating. It respects the child's developmental pace rather than trying to outrun it.

Our first child started there, and it showed. Vocabulary came early. Engagement was calm.

## Graduating Up: When Simple Isn't Enough

But children grow fast, and good content should grow with them. What we found next came largely from the BBC - specifically the CBeebies library - and the quality jump was striking.

**[Super Simple Songs](https://www.youtube.com/@SuperSimpleSongs)** became a household staple through 2025 - a solid middle tier. The music is clean, the animations are calm, and the repetition is structured rather than frantic. Their spinoff **[Noodle & Pals](https://www.youtube.com/@NoodleAndPals)** became a good representation of our family's vibe - gentle, warm, [musically satisfying](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wqvQAcloTRQ). **[The Bumble Nums](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=P09Ev4FNYo4)**, another Super Simple property, deserves a mention too - cooking adventures with a simple recurring structure that toddlers love.

On the BBC side, we stumbled onto some of the claymation productions - **[The Very Small Creatures](https://www.aardman.com/film-tv-games/the-very-small-creatures/)** from Aardman (the studio behind Wallace & Gromit and Morph). My first reaction was skepticism. It looked handmade and unhurried. But that's exactly the point. The tactile, physical quality of stop-motion holds attention differently than flat digital animation - it signals a world with texture and weight, not just color and noise.

The shows that really won us over, though, were **[Numberblocks](https://youtu.be/8ZbAH6QWSmA?si=iT_tuEF38PzsaeS_&t=157)** and **[Alphablocks](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JDOVK-oyu6M)**. On the surface, these are modest productions - simple character designs, not a lot of dramatic action. But structurally, they're extraordinary. Each episode is a self-contained concept lesson. Numberblocks teaches genuine mathematical intuition: grouping, decomposition, multiplication as area. Alphablocks teaches phonics through play. My child didn't just learn to recognize letters - she started *sounding them out*, because the show modeled the process. There's a real curriculum embedded here, and it's delivered without condescension and without chaos.

## The Case Against Overstimulation

<!-- TODO: this section is the most essay-like. Consider whether the slot machine analogy and "colonized their attention" language is yours or the AI's. The core argument is strong - you clearly feel this - but the phrasing needs your voice. -->

I want to spend some time on what I think is the central problem in children's media, because it's not just a matter of taste - there's something philosophically important at stake.

**Cocomelon** is the most prominent example of what I'd call *attention-capture-first* design. It is engineered, consciously or not, around the same principles that make slot machines compelling: rapid scene changes, relentless high-saturation color, musical loops with no resolution, characters who react with exaggerated affect to everything. The result is a child who is *engaged* in the way a gambler is engaged - not learning, not exploring, not connecting, but transfixed. The screen has colonized their attention rather than invited it.

The research on this is still developing, but the behavioral signs are intuitive to any parent who has tried to turn it off: inconsolable upset disproportionate to the situation, difficulty transitioning to quieter activities, a shortened baseline for tolerating low-stimulation environments. We observed this with other children in our circle before we even knew to worry about it.

This isn't about screen time in aggregate - it's about *the quality of what the screen is doing to the brain that's watching it*. Cocomelon-style content optimizes for retention at the cost of depth. It leaves a child wanting more of the same rather than curious about what comes next.

**[Bebefinn](https://www.youtube.com/channel/UC-cY4X2sLECUhishGNiyofQ)** sits in an interesting middle ground. The production values are decent, the songs are catchy, and some of the content is genuinely educational. It's better than the chaos-tier content but doesn't quite reach the intentional rigor of the BBC productions. A reasonable fallback; not a first choice.

## What Makes Children's Content Good? A Framework

After a few years of watching alongside our child - and now starting the whole journey again with a newborn - here's how I've come to think about it:

**Pacing respects the child's timeline.** Good shows wait. They repeat. They give a child time to respond before the next thing happens. This is true of Miss Rachel, Numberblocks, and the best of the BBC library. It mirrors how attentive adults actually talk to children.

**Stimulation serves meaning, not retention.** Color, sound, and movement should illuminate the concept being taught - not exist as their own reward. Numberblocks uses color to represent quantity. That's purposeful. Cocomelon uses color to keep the eyes moving. That's manipulative.

**There is a there there.** The best content introduces the child to a *world* with internal logic. Numberblocks characters have personalities that reflect their mathematical properties. Alphablocks have sounds that are their names. When children engage with these shows, they're not just watching - they're building a mental model.

**It doesn't talk down.** The worst children's content assumes children can't follow anything without constant reinforcement through volume and speed. The best assumes children are capable of attention, nuance, and even some confusion - which is where curiosity lives.

**It holds up to rewatching by an adult.** This is a practical test. If you're going to watch something thirty times, it shouldn't make you feel lobotomized by episode three.

## Where We Are Now

With our newborn, we're being more deliberate from the start. The first months won't involve screens at all - the research there is fairly clear that passive screen exposure under six months offers nothing and potentially competes with the face-to-face interaction that's doing the real developmental work. But when we do introduce content, we know what we're reaching for: slow, musical, concept-driven, tactile-feeling, made by people who seemed to actually think about children when they made it.

Miss Rachel will come back. So will Numberblocks. We'll look harder at the deeper BBC catalog. And we'll be more vigilant about the stuff that looks like it's for children but is really for the algorithm.

What I've come to believe is that children's entertainment is one of the clearest cases where *taste has ethics*. Choosing what your child watches is a small act of values-setting about attention, learning, and what the world is supposed to feel like. That's worth thinking carefully about.
